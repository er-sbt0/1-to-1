Nash-Williams / Tutte · tree packing · 1961

Three islands,
three bridges — sparsity hides at an intermediate resolution

A spanning tree must spend exactly r − 1 of its edges to weld r clusters of vertices into one piece. So k edge-disjoint spanning trees need at least k(r − 1) edges running between clusters — for every way of clustering. The miracle of the theorem is that this obvious toll is the only toll.

This page is about the word every. You will meet a graph that pays the toll at the coarsest resolution (r = 2, the cuts) and pays it at the finest (r = n, the headcount) — and is still bankrupt in between.

02 — instinct one

“Just make it k-edge-connected”

The r = 2 case of the condition is exactly k-edge-connectivity: a 2-part clustering is a cut, and it needs k(2 − 1) = k edges. That is such a natural condition that it feels like the whole story. It is not.

Take the 6-cycle and k = 2. Every one of its 31 cuts has at least 2 edges — C6 is genuinely 2-edge-connected, so it passes the r = 2 test. But C6 is small enough that we can enumerate every single partition of its vertex set — all Bell(6) = 203 of them — and read off the true minimum cross-edge count at each resolution.

C6 is a warm-up, not the punchline. It fails at r = 3 (3 cross edges < 4 required), but it also fails the plain headcount at r = n: 6 edges < 2·5 = 10. So a sceptic can patch the instinct — “check the cuts and check the edge count” — and C6 is caught.

Cut the fifteen vertices into the three islands. Every K5 is internally lavish — but only three edges leave their island, and welding three clusters into one costs each tree two edges, so two trees need four. Three is less than four. No two edge-disjoint spanning trees exist. The obstruction is invisible at r = 2 and invisible at r = 15; it lives only at r = 3.

06 — what is sufficient

Double-count your way out: 2k-edge-connectivity

Checking every partition is expensive. But there is a cheap condition that implies all of them at once — and its proof is one line of double counting.

no contradiction — read this carefully

The one-bridge island graph is 2-edge-connected, and for k = 2 the corollary asks for 4-edge-connectivity. It misses the hypothesis by a factor of two, so it never contradicted the corollary — it only refutes the weaker instinct that k-edge-connectivity is enough.

Doubling the bridges makes the minimum cut 4 = 2k, the corollary applies, and the packer really does find the two trees.

Also note the corollary is not tight at k = 1: it would demand 2-edge-connectivity, but plain connectivity already gives you one spanning tree. The factor 2 is the price of a uniform statement.

07 — sharpness

2k cannot be lowered to 2k − 1

For k = 2 the corollary asks for 4-edge-connectivity. Three-edge-connectivity is not enough, and the Petersen graph says so — while also giving a second, independent example of an obstruction hiding away from r = 2.

Petersen n = 10, m = 15, 3-edge-connected
exhaustive scan Petersen · all Bell(10) = 115 975 partitions
two lessons from one graph

Sharpness. Petersen is (2k − 1)-edge-connected for k = 2 but has only 15 edges where 2·9 = 18 are needed. So (2k − 1)-edge-connectivity does not imply k edge-disjoint spanning trees, and the 2k in the corollary is best possible.

Resolution, again. Look at where the exhaustive scan first dips below the line. It is not r = 10. Petersen is already short at r = 8, two resolutions before the headcount notices anything.

08 — the essence

What the theorem actually says

A packing of k spanning trees can fail for exactly one reason: some way of clustering the vertices is too sparsely stitched together. Not “too few edges” — the island graph has plenty. Not “a thin cut” — the island graph's cuts are all wide enough. A clustering, at some particular number of parts, whose crossing edges cannot pay k(r − 1).

That is why the condition quantifies over every r, and why you cannot sample the ends of the range and go home. Sparsity is not a property of a graph at one scale. It is a property of a graph at a resolution, and it can hide in the middle.
