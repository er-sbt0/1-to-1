Nash-Williams & Tutte, 1961 — tree packing

Two trees in K4.
And the excuse when it can't be done.

Paint every edge of a graph with k colors so that each color, by itself, is a spanning tree. Sometimes you can. Sometimes you fight for an hour and fail.

The theorem below says the failure is never mysterious. Whenever a packing is impossible there is a one-line excuse: some way of clustering the vertices that leaves too few edges running between clusters. Nothing else can ever go wrong.

You can get agonisingly close. Petersen certainly has a spanning tree, and it has a spanning tree plus a disjoint spanning forest — press "best of 3000" and watch color 2 fill up with 6 edges stuck in 4 separate pieces. What it never has is a second edge-disjoint tree.

Honesty Your failure to find a packing is not a proof that none exists — you might just be bad at it, and so might a greedy algorithm. The exhaustive search above is a proof, but it is a brute-force one: 5005 cases and no insight. What follows is the insight.
04 — the excuse

The certificate is one line of arithmetic

Cluster the Petersen graph's vertices in the crudest possible way: every vertex alone in its own cluster. Then every edge is a "between-clusters" edge, and the count settles it.

Petersen, shattered into 10 singleton clusters
what the numbers are, counted from the graph

Fifteen edges cannot pay for eighteen. That is the whole argument, and you can check it without ever drawing a tree. "I can't do it" has become "and here is exactly why."

The same line also kills a tempting shortcut. Petersen is 3-edge-connected — you must delete 3 edges to disconnect it — yet it has no 2 edge-disjoint spanning trees. So "(2k−1)-edge-connected" is not enough for k trees. (2k-edge-connected is enough; that is a corollary at the bottom of this page. Petersen misses it by one.)

05 — the essence

Every way of clustering the vertices charges a toll

Squash each cluster down to a single point. A spanning tree survives the squashing as something that still connects all r points — so it must spend at least r − 1 of its edges on the wires running between clusters. k disjoint trees must therefore spend k(r − 1). The miracle of the theorem is that this obvious toll is the only toll there is.

Try it on K₄ with the real 2-packing loaded: whatever clustering you build, each color still crosses it at least r−1 times. It has no choice — a tree that connects everything must connect the clusters too. That inequality, summed over the k colors, is the necessary direction of the theorem, and it takes about ten seconds to believe.

The hard direction — the one Nash-Williams and Tutte proved independently in 1961 — is that nothing else can obstruct you. If every single clustering pays its toll, the k trees exist.

06 — why "all partitions" really means all

Passing the edge count is not enough

Petersen failed at r = n — a mere headcount of edges. That makes it a friendly example and a misleading one. Here is a graph with plenty of edges, whose every cut is fine, that still fails — at an intermediate clustering.

Three copies of K₅, joined in a triangle by 3 edges

Each K₅ is dense enough to look generous, and each has two edges leaving it, so no single cut is starved. But squash the three blobs to three points and only 3 wires remain, where two disjoint spanning trees would need 4. The obstruction lives at r = 3 and nowhere else — invisible to the total edge count, invisible to edge-connectivity.

So the quantifier is load-bearing The condition is "for every partition", over all r from 1 to n. Checking only r = n (the edge count) or only r = 2 (the cuts) can pass a graph that has no packing. The theorem's content is that once you have checked them all, you are done — there is no further, subtler obstruction hiding behind them.
07 — the statement

Written out, and what it buys you

the two directions

Necessity (easy, shown in §5). Contract each part. A spanning tree becomes a connected spanning subgraph of the r-point quotient, so it uses ≥ r−1 cross edges. The k trees are edge-disjoint, so together they use ≥ k(r−1) distinct cross edges. Hence a packing forces the inequality.

Sufficiency (hard, 1961). If every partition pays, the packing exists. Modern proofs run through matroid union: the graphic matroid's union with itself k times has rank k(n−1) exactly when the partition condition holds. No hand-waving substitute is known.

Three things not to over-claim · Petersen's obstruction is the finest partition, detected by a headcount. Most obstructions are not that easy — §6 is the honest picture. · Failing to find a packing by hand, or by a greedy algorithm, proves nothing. Only a partition certificate (or a complete search) does. · "Edge-disjoint" means the trees share no edge. They share every vertex — a spanning tree, by definition, touches all of V.
