The glue budget at every zoom level

A spanning tree is glue. Squint at a graph until only r clusters of vertices are visible, and any one spanning tree must spend exactly r−1 of its edges running between clusters — no fewer, or the clusters would not be welded into one piece. So k edge-disjoint spanning trees have to pay a toll of crossing edges, at every possible way of clustering.

Nash-Williams and Tutte proved the miracle: that obvious toll is the only toll. Nothing else can go wrong. The sole way tree-packing can fail is for some clustering of the vertices to be too sparsely stitched together.

Why the toll is exactly

Contract every part of a partition down to a single supernode. A spanning tree of becomes a connected spanning submultigraph of the -vertex quotient, so at least of its edges survive the contraction — those are precisely its cross edges. The trees are edge-disjoint, so their cross edges are disjoint too, and the counts simply add.

02

Descend the tower of resolutions

Every level of this tower is one resolution. The top is : every vertex alone, nothing merged, every edge crossing. The bottom is : two clusters — that is, a cut. Descending merges clusters, so interior edges vanish and the cross count falls. But the toll falls too, along a perfectly straight line. The entire theorem is the race between those two.

Beside the tower hangs the budget ladder: at each level a bar for the cross edges actually present, and a tick for the toll owed. Read the ladder edge-on and it is a chart; look down the tower and it is a graph collapsing — one object, two readings. Scroll to descend, or drag the resolution slider.

04

Honest caveat: “every zoom level” is not a chain

A scrubber suggests a tidy hierarchy — level a coarsening of level , one path down. The theorem quantifies over far more than that. At each fixed there are different partitions (a Stirling number of the second kind) and the condition must hold for every single one. So what actually matters at each resolution is the minimum over that entire cloud.

Below, the cloud is drawn. The orange line is the tower's nested chain — one path. The blue line is the true minimum at each . They are not the same line, and the theorem cares only about the blue one. Switch the tower to minimisers mode and this becomes visible in 3-D: the connecting trunks disappear, because the optimal partition at is generally not a coarsening of the optimal partition at .

05

When the budget holds everywhere, the glue materialises

That is the deep half, and this page asserts it rather than proving it: if every partition clears its toll, the edge-disjoint spanning trees genuinely exist. Here they are — computed by a real matroid-union packing (greedy growth plus a breadth-first exchange search over edges), then peeled apart into separate layers so you can see that no edge is shared.

Concretely: edge-disjoint spanning trees are independent communication backbones on one set of wires. Lose an entire backbone and the network is still connected by each of the others.

06

What is asserted here, and what is computed

Asserted, not proved

Sufficiency. That passing every partition test forces the trees to exist is the deep half (Tutte 1961; Nash-Williams 1961), and nothing on this page argues for it. The necessity direction, §1, is proved here in three lines.

For the sufficiency argument as an algorithm — a packing that either produces the trees or emits the violating partition as a certificate — see the companion artifact 04 · certificate duality.

The scrubber is a simplification

The tower shows one nested chain, partitions. The theorem quantifies over all of them (). §4 draws the rest of the cloud, and minimisers mode shows that the per- optima do not nest — the trunks vanish because there is no hierarchy connecting them.

No verdict here comes from the chain. A failing verdict comes from one exhibited partition, recounted from scratch; a passing verdict comes from exhibited trees, re-verified from scratch. Neither needs the search to be optimal.
