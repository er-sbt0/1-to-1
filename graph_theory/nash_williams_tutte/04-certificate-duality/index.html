Nash-Williams · Tutte, 1961 — the hard direction

A stuck packing writes its own excuse

Necessity is a one-line count. Sufficiency is the entire theorem — and its real content is not “the trees exist.” It is this: when they don’t exist, the algorithm that fails hands you the reason it failed. Below, a real packing algorithm runs on a real graph. When it gets stuck, the wreckage of its search folds into a partition of the vertices that is provably short of edges — and you can check the arithmetic yourself, without trusting a single thing the algorithm did.

01

The price of welding

Chop into clusters and squint until each cluster is a single dot. A spanning tree must connect those dots. Every crossing edge it uses can merge at most two pieces into one, so it must spend at least of them. edge-disjoint trees spend edges no other tree may reuse. So the graph must stock at least crossing edges — for every clustering at once.

And now the asymmetry. Nothing in that paragraph hints that a sparse clustering is the only way to fail. A graph could conceivably be tangled in some other, subtler way. The theorem says it cannot. Everything below is about why.

Trace

Read the failure honestly. If you scrub to the end of a stuck run, all you have watched is one algorithm giving up. That is not a proof of anything — a different edge order, a different tie-break, a cleverer heuristic might have done better. The proof is the object the failure leaves behind.

        Two things the page refuses to say

        1. “The greedy got stuck, therefore it is impossible.” False, and never claimed. A stuck run is a prompt to produce a certificate, nothing more. Only the partition, recounted, settles it.

        2. “This animation is the proof.” It is a faithful rendering of the algorithm and an honest rendering of the certificate construction. The lemma that ties them together is quoted, and flagged as quoted, above.
