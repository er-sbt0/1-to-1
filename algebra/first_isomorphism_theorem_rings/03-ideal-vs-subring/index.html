First Isomorphism Theorem · Rings · the subtlety

The kernel must be an ideal, not merely a subring

The theorem says R/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi. For the left side to even be a ring, coset multiplication has to be well-defined — and that is exactly the absorption property of an ideal. Here you can watch it work for a real ideal, and break for a mere subring.

The claim we stress-test
R/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi \qquad\text{where}\qquad \ker\varphi=\{x\in R: \varphi(x)=0\}.

Why is R/\ker\varphi guaranteed to be a ring? Because \ker\varphi is always an ideal. Remove that property and the quotient falls apart.

STEP 1 · THE DEFINITION

Additive subgroup is easy. Absorption is the hard part.

A subset I\subseteq R is a (two-sided) ideal when both hold:

\textbf{(A)}\quad (I,+)\ \text{is a subgroup of}\ (R,+)
\textbf{(B)}\quad \forall\, r\in R,\ \forall\, x\in I:\quad r\cdot x\in I\ \ \text{and}\ \ x\cdot r\in I

(A) alone is enough to build the quotient group R/I under addition. Multiplication of cosets is the extra promise — and it needs (B), absorption.

Why \ker\varphi always passes both

Let \varphi:R\to S be a ring homomorphism and x\in\ker\varphi, so \varphi(x)=0. For any r\in R:

\varphi(rx)=\varphi(r)\,\varphi(x)=\varphi(r)\cdot 0 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; rx\in\ker\varphi.

Absorption is automatic for a kernel. That is the whole reason the theorem is phrased about kernels — they are never "just" subrings.

STEP 2 · WHERE ABSORPTION IS FORCED

Coset multiplication, honestly

Define (a+I)(b+I) := ab+I. To be a real operation this must not depend on which representatives we pick. Take other representatives a'=a+i, b'=b+j with i,j\in I and expand:

a'b' = (a+i)(b+j) = ab + \underbrace{aj + ib + ij}_{\textstyle \text{must live in } I}.
a'b' \in ab + I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad aj+ib+ij \in I \ \ \text{for all } a,b\in R,\ i,j\in I.

That condition — aj,\ ib,\ ij \in I for every a,b — is precisely absorption. So:

\text{Coset multiplication is well-defined} \iff I \text{ absorbs products} \iff I \text{ is an ideal.}

No hand-waving: if even one of aj,ib,ij escapes I, two legitimate representative choices give products in different cosets, and R/I has no consistent multiplication.

STEP 4 · THE CONTRAST

Same ring, two subsets, opposite fates

Ideal  I=\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}

Subgroup? yes  ·  Absorbs? yes  →  ideal.

r\cdot(0,b)=(r_1\!\cdot\!0,\ r_2 b)=(0,\ r_2b)\in I. Coset multiplication is unambiguous, so R/I is a genuine ring.

I really is a kernel: the projection \varphi(x,y)=x has \ker\varphi=I, and

R/I \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}.

The theorem applies, exactly as advertised.

Subring only  \Delta=\{(a,a)\}

Subgroup? yes  ·  Closed under \times? yes  ·  Absorbs? no.

Witness: (1,0)\cdot(1,1)=(1,0)\notin\Delta. It is a bona-fide subring but not an ideal.

Coset multiplication is ambiguous: e.g. with the coset [(0,1)],

(0,1)(0,1)=(0,1)\in[(0,1)],\quad (1,2)(1,2)=(1,1)\in[\Delta].

Two valid representatives, two different result cosets — R/\Delta is not a ring, and "\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi" is meaningless.

STEP 5 · THE PUNCHLINE

Why the theorem never trips over this

You cannot hand the First Isomorphism Theorem a "bad" subring, because you never start from a subring — you start from a homomorphism. And every kernel absorbs products:

x\in\ker\varphi \ \Rightarrow\ \varphi(rx)=\varphi(r)\varphi(x)=0 \ \Rightarrow\ rx\in\ker\varphi \quad(\text{and likewise } xr).

So \ker\varphi is always an ideal, coset multiplication is always well-defined, R/\ker\varphi is always a ring, and the induced map

\bar\varphi: R/\ker\varphi \to \operatorname{im}\varphi,\qquad \bar\varphi(x+\ker\varphi)=\varphi(x)

is a well-defined ring isomorphism. The absorption you watched break for \Delta is the very thing a kernel guarantees for free.

Ideal ⇒ quotient is a ring. Subring-only ⇒ multiplication ambiguous. The theorem lives on the right side of that line.
