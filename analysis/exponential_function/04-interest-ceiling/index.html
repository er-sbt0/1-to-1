The interest-rate ceiling:
growth in slow motion

A bank pays 100% interest a year. You deposit $1. If the bank compounds twice a year instead of once, you end with $2.25 instead of $2.00. Four times: $2.44. Daily: $2.7146. So — compound continuously, infinitely often, and you get… infinite money?

No. You get $2.71828…, and not one cent more. This page is about why that ceiling exists, and about the much bigger thing hiding underneath it: the discrete compounding process is the law “rate = current amount” written in slow motion. Taking the slices to zero does not build a new object. It takes the limit of the same law. The function comes first; the number e is what falls out.

03

The bridge: where the factorials come from

This is the heart of the matter. Expand with the binomial theorem and something remarkable falls out — the finite compounding product is the power series, term by term, each term damped by a bracket that creeps up to 1.

Two things follow immediately, and they are the two things you actually needed to know:

(1) Why the balance always goes up with n. Every bracket increases with n, and a larger n adds more terms to the sum. Nothing ever decreases. That is the monotonicity — no calculus required.

Honesty: one step here is asserted, not proved
Passing from “each term converges” to “the sum converges to the sum of the limits” is an interchange of two limits ( and the infinite summation) and it is not automatic — there are sequences of series where it fails. Here it is legitimate: the terms are non-negative and increase in n, so monotone convergence applies (dominated convergence works too, with dominating series ). This page shows you the numbers doing it; it does not prove it.

05

Time adds, money multiplies

Grow for x years, then grow for y more. Same as growing for x + y. Obvious about money — and it is the entire functional equation .

The beautiful part: with a fixed slice length , this identity is already exactly true before any limit. Growing for m slices and then k more is — nothing but the schoolbook law of integer exponents. The limit inherits it. That is why the exponential turns addition into multiplication: it was doing so at every finite stage.

Catastrophic cancellation, family resemblance
This is the same disease as the difference-quotient noise floor: a tiny quantity added to 1, then the 1 taken back out. Whenever your formula computes and then depends on , doubles will betray you.

Both constructions define the same function. They are not remotely the same algorithm.

Error for compounding is — first order, a straight line of slope on this log-log plot. Error for the truncated series is — it falls off the bottom of the chart. To buy ten correct digits by compounding you need roughly 2.7 × 10¹⁰ compoundings; the series needs 14 terms.

So why teach compounding at all? Because it is the definition that explains itself: it is the law “rate = amount” executed in slow motion, in units anyone can hold in their hand. The series is the fast way to compute; this is the honest way to see.

07

What actually happened here

We never assumed a number e. We wrote down one rule — the increase over a slice of time is the current amount times the length of the slice — and executed it n times. That is , and it is Euler's method for .

Refining the slices does not change the rule; it only stops rounding it. The limit exists because the sequence is increasing (every binomial bracket climbs toward 1) and bounded (every bracket is at most 1, and ). The limit is the power series, because that is where the brackets take you. And the limit turns sums into products, because was true at every finite stage.

Only at the very end, by evaluating at , does a number appear: . The function came first. The number is the byproduct. And the ceiling is not a disappointment — it is the whole content of the theorem. Compounding faster buys you more, with brutally diminishing returns, and the thing it is diminishing toward is the one motion whose speed always equals its position.
